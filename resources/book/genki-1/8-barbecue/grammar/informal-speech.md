# Informal Speech

Close friends and family members end sentences with short forms as a sign of intimacy. Using long forms, by contrast, implies the speaker wants to "keep a proper distance." Short forms are like talking on a first-name basis; long forms are like using "Mr."/"Ms."

## Seniority complicates the switch
Japanese speakers are very conscious of relative seniority. Even a year's age difference can preclude a truly "equal" relationship, and permission to use short forms isn't necessarily mutual — a senior partner may use short forms with you while still expecting long forms in return. If your professor speaks to you in short forms, they'd be surprised if you replied the same way.

## Grammar notes specific to casual speech
- Question sentences drop the question particle か, relying on rising intonation alone: どんな音楽（おんがく）を聞（き）く？("What kind of music do you listen to?") — not ×どんな音楽を聞く か？
- The だ ending of な-adjectives/noun＋です (see [[short-forms]]) is usually dropped at the very end of a sentence, or followed by ね／よ: メアリーさんは二年生（にねんせい）。or メアリーさんは二年生だよ。— rather than メアリーさんは二年生だ。
- はい／いいえ are usually replaced by the less formal うん／ううん in casual conversation.
